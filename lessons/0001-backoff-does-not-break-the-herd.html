Backoff spaces the herd out. Only jitter breaks it up.

Lesson 1 · the one idea behind the word you heard in the rant

A service hiccups. Sixty clients get an error at the same instant. Every one of them is a well-behaved citizen running textbook exponential backoff, so every one of them waits — politely, patiently — and retries.

At exactly the same moment as all the others.

That is the whole problem in one sentence, and it is the thing most people get wrong about backoff. Doubling the delay makes the herd rarer. It does not make the herd smaller. Sixty clients that failed together will fail together again, and again, because they are all computing the same number from the same formula at the same time. They are correlated, and no amount of doubling removes correlation.

Jitter is the fix: deliberately randomising each client's wait so the fleet stops moving as one block. Below is a simulator. Don't take the claim on trust — go break it.

Micro-world: the retry storm

Retry storm simulator

Sixty clients all hit a struggling server at tick 0. The server accepts only capacity requests per tick; everyone else is rejected and backs off. Largest retry burst is the one to watch — it counts how many clients still land on a single tick after the initial hit, which is exactly how intact the herd still is. Things to try
  1. Run it as-is, on No jitter. A handful of tall, isolated spikes with dead air between them, dragging on to tick 760. The largest retry burst is 56 of 60: after all that backing off, the herd is still almost perfectly intact.
  2. Change only the strategy to Full jitter. Same seed, same everything. Completion drops from 760 to 30, total calls from 480 to 145, and the largest retry burst from 56 to 12. Nothing got faster per client — the block just stopped arriving as a block.
  3. Press Compare all four. Note that Equal jitter finishes at 52 while Full finishes at 30: keeping a guaranteed half of the delay keeps half the correlation too. That is the trade-off the name hides.
  4. Now set capacity to 60 so nobody is ever throttled, and try every strategy. They all finish on tick 0 and jitter changes nothing at all. Work out why — the answer is what jitter is for.
  5. Back to capacity 4. On Full jitter, change only the seed a few times: the shape wobbles, the outcome barely moves. On No jitter, the seed does nothing whatsoever. Why not?

What the four strategies actually compute

Marc Brooker named these in the 2015 AWS post that made jitter standard practice. All four pick a sleep duration after a failed attempt; they differ only in how much randomness they let in.

Notice what changes across the list: not the size of the delay, but how much of it is predictable. The no-jitter line contains no randomness at all, which is precisely why every client lands on the same tick. Equal jitter is the instinctive compromise — "surely we should still guarantee a minimum wait?" — and it is measurably the worst of the three jittered options, because that guaranteed half is exactly the correlated part you were trying to destroy.

"But full jitter can pick a sleep of nearly zero — isn't that rude to a struggling server?" One client retrying immediately is not the problem. Sixty clients retrying simultaneously is. Full jitter trades a slightly rude individual for a fleet that never arrives as a block, and Brooker's simulations show it wins on both total work and completion time.

What this lesson is deliberately not claiming

Jitter breaks up correlation. That is all it does. It does not reduce the total amount of work a client fleet will eventually send, and if clients keep arriving indefinitely, spreading retries out just defers load rather than shedding it. Brooker's own 2022 follow-up is blunt about this: the only cure for sustained overload is to reduce load. Jitter is the right tool for a spike, not for a system that is simply too small.

Cold-recall defense (one breath):
Exponential backoff makes retries rarer but keeps them synchronised — every client that failed together computes the identical delay and comes back together. Jitter randomises the wait so the fleet decorrelates. It buys nothing when there's no contention, and it defers rather than removes load. Default to full jitter; equal jitter preserves the very correlation you were trying to kill.
